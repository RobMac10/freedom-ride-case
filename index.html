20 February 1965

Moree

Egg on my sleeve. I keep wiping it. Still there.

We came back. We had to. Last time they said the ban was off. We left. They put it back on. I don’t know why I thought a meeting would be enough.

The crowd was already at the pool. Pressing in. So hot I could hardly breathe. Someone spat. Then the tomatoes, the eggs. I have never been looked at like that.

I keep thinking we don’t live here. We’re from Sydney. We get on the bus. The children don’t.

The police said if we stayed it would get worse. We stayed. I wanted to go. I hate that I wanted to go.

Charlie wouldn’t move from the gate. They grabbed him. For a moment I couldn’t see the kids and I thought —

I thought I would be braver than this.

One of the children was knocked down. They were frightened. So was I. What a thing to come all this way and still be frightened.

They let them in, in the end. We went in too. For a minute it felt like winning. Then we had to walk to the bus in a single line and they were still throwing things and the windows kept getting hit.

A man at the bus said it quietly.

They’ll wait till you’re gone.

— on the bus

CASE FILE: FREEDOM RIDE / 1965

Date of incident
12–26 February 1965 (Moree visits: 16 and 19–20 February)
Location
Regional New South Wales, including Walgett and Moree
Status
Unresolved contradictions · Draft, pending consultation

Key claims still disputed

  • What actually changed in the towns, and for how long?
  • Whose record should we trust — student diaries, local Aboriginal accounts, or the 1965 newspapers and television?
  • When was the Moree baths ban really lifted?

What is agreed · from the 1965 file, and after

  • Twenty-nine University of Sydney students left around midnight on 12 February 1965, as Student Action for Aborigines (SAFA). Charles Perkins, an Arrernte man, was president. Some left later and others joined.
  • They travelled through regional New South Wales for about two weeks, using surveys, pickets and cameras, and they kept diaries. Newspapers, radio and television reported the trip as it happened.
  • They went to Moree twice. On 16 February, Aboriginal children were at first refused the baths, then let in. That evening a public meeting voted 88 to 10 to remove the segregation clause.
  • After they left, the old 1955 rule was enforced again. They returned on 19–20 February. A hostile crowd met them at the pool. Eggs, tomatoes and stones were thrown. Children and students went in, then left that night through the crowd.
  • Local Aboriginal people were already campaigning in towns such as Walgett. The students met Harry Hall, president of the Walgett Aborigines’ Progressive Association, on 15 February.
  • The Moree baths rule used the period language of racial exclusion. The wording is in Super Sleuths, with a content note. The ban was not settled in a single day.

Green on this page marks what can be agreed. Amber and other colours, used in Super Sleuths, mark what is still disputed or was added later.

Three witnesses

The same three questions for each: What did they say they saw? Could they really have seen it? Why might they say it? Each has a plain silhouette and a name card. No likeness of a real person has been invented.

A note on the language of the records

Some 1965 records, and some later interviews, use racist language. This file does not repeat slurs on the student pages. A short extract from the 1955 Moree baths rule is in Super Sleuths, clearly attributed, with a content note. It is not the language of this case file.

Charles Perkins

Arrernte man · president of Student Action for Aborigines

A public statement after the ride, and a later account of the return to Moree.

Source. The line “The problem is out in the open now” is quoted by AIATSIS as said to about 200 people at the 1965 FCAATSI conference in Canberra. His book A Bastard Like Me (1975) describes the second Moree visit. Perkins died in 2000.

What they said. After the ride he told a national Aboriginal conference, “The problem is out in the open now.” Of the return to Moree he later wrote of a hostile crowd, and of getting the children in, then walking them back to the bus.

Could they really have seen it? He was on the bus from Sydney and at both Moree visits. He stood at the pool gate on the return. He was not a neutral observer: he led the group and wanted the colour bar broken and the story reported.

Why might they say it? To show that the ride had forced a public reckoning, and to keep pressure on the towns after the bus left. A book written years later is memory, not a 1965 diary.

Your notes

Ann Curthoys

University of Sydney student on the bus · diarist

A diary kept on the trip, later published.

Source. Ann Curthoys, diary of the 1965 Freedom Ride, AIATSIS collection; published in Freedom Ride: A Freedom Rider Remembers (2002). Living — no dates given here.

What they said. Her diary of 16 February records a first visit to the Moree baths and an evening meeting that voted 88 to 10 to remove the segregation clause. Two days later she recorded that the old rule was being enforced again. Of the return she wrote that she had “never met such hostile, hate-filled people,” and that the hostility seemed aimed at the students as “university student intruders.” She noted eggs, tomatoes and stones, frightened children, and leaving in single file.

Could they really have seen it? She was on the bus and at both Moree visits. A diary written at the time is close to the events, but it is still one student’s view. She could not see everything in a crowd.

Why might they say it? She was keeping a record for herself, and later she published it as history. She was a city student on a protest, not a local resident.

Your notes

Harry Hall

President of the Walgett Aborigines’ Progressive Association

A local Aboriginal leader the students met in Walgett, before Moree.

Source. Named in accounts of the 1965 Walgett campaign, including the AIATSIS Freedom Ride page and reporting of the later 1965 colour-bar fights. He is deceased. This card is not a 1965 first-person diary. Do not confuse him with his son, who is living and is not this witness.

What they said. The 1965 record names him as president of the Walgett association, and as a person the students met when they reached the town on 15 February. Local campaigning against segregation was already underway. Later that year he asked Perkins and other activists to come back and help fight the colour bar at the Oasis Hotel.

Could they really have seen it? He lived in Walgett and had been campaigning there before the bus arrived. He was not at the Moree pool on 20 February. Much of what later family and community accounts add comes after 1965.

Why might they say it? To record that the ride did not start the fight in Walgett, and to get outside help when the colour bar was still in place after the bus had gone. Later memory is not the same thing as a 1965 statement.

The two Moree visits

The written rule at the baths

Content note

The 1955 Moree baths rule used racist period language. It is quoted here only as a short, attributed extract, with an ellipsis where the source shortens the list of premises. It is not this file’s language.

A widely cited wording, quoted by ABC News in 2025 from the local rule, begins: “No person being a full blooded or half cast Aboriginal of Australia or being a person apparently having admixture of Aboriginal blood, shall use, occupy or be present in … the premises.” The statute is often dated 6 June 1955.

Who wrote a rule like that, and who had to live under it? Ann Curthoys later recalled that it took about six months before the council formally rescinded it. The mayor’s words on 20 February and the council’s later decision are not the same thing.

Who was already campaigning?

The students met Harry Hall in Walgett on 15 February. Local campaigning against segregation was already underway there. Later that year Hall asked Perkins and others to come back and help fight the colour bar at the Oasis Hotel.

Why might a student diary and a local leader’s later campaign tell different parts of the story?

To take further
  • Twenty-nine students left Sydney. About 35 took part in all, because some left and others joined. Why might those two figures both be true?
  • Gary Williams, a Gumbaynggirr-Bundjalung student and a member of SAFA, was not on the bus from Sydney. He joined at Bowraville after a family emergency, after both Moree visits. What does that do to a story that treats “the students” as one group?

For teachers

Teacher notes

These notes are not for the student page. The notebook is written to look authentic; what is true about it is here.

The opener

The notebook is constructed. It is not an original 1965 page, and the handwriting is not original. That fact belongs only in this teacher note, not on the student page. The writer is an unnamed city student on the bus, in the spirit of diaries kept on the trip; the page is not Ann Curthoys’s diary and must not be taught as hers.

The date is 20 February 1965, the second Moree visit, after the first meeting and after the ban was put back on. Scene details follow Ann Curthoys’s contemporary diary (AIATSIS collection; published 2002) and Charles Perkins’s later account of the return, as quoted on the AIATSIS Freedom Ride page.

The source draft’s “four nights ago,” the 16 February date, the SAFA title, the method-explaining paragraph, the 88-to-10 vote, the by-law wording, the number aboard, Gary Williams, and Lismore were taken off this page. Two invented conversations — a twelve-year-old who had never been inside, and a woman from the mission — were cut.

Two scene lines were checked against the 20 February record and kept. “Charlie wouldn’t move from the gate. They grabbed him.” Curthoys’s diary of the return, as quoted by AIATSIS, records: “Then he went to the front of the line and when he refused to move was grabbed and taken away from the line.” Perkins, in A Bastard Like Me (1975), also quoted by AIATSIS, places the struggle at the gate: “The mayor ordered the police to have us removed from the gate entrance. They took hold of my arm and the struggle started.” The notebook’s “from the gate” is a short compression of those two accounts of the same confrontation. “One of the children was knocked down.” Curthoys’s diary, as quoted by AIATSIS, records: “One child had been knocked down. The poor kids were very frightened.” She wrote that the children told the students this, and that they had also been subjected to racist abuse (the wording is not repeated on the student pages). Contemporary newspaper headlines of 21–22 February 1965 report a violent confrontation at Moree; this file did not independently recover those two exact phrases from a Trove article body.

The closing is now: “A man at the bus said it quietly.” then “They’ll wait till you’re gone.” Both the speaker and the line are constructed. The man is not a named historical person. Do not teach either as a documented 1965 quotation. The line stands in for a documented pattern: after the first visit the ban was reimposed once the bus had left, and later Moree memory records a similar fear. In 2025, Gomeroi man Uncle Darryl French, who was on the bus to the pool on the return visit, recalled people saying that when the students left, local people would have to deal with it (ABC News, 22 February 2025). Living people, including French, are not given dates on the student pages.

Corrections and flags

Two Moree visits, not one. First visit, 16 February: children at first refused, then let in; evening meeting 88 to 10 (Curthoys’s diary, via AIATSIS; ABC News, 2025). After the students left, the 1955 statute was enforced again (Curthoys, 18 February entry). Return, 19–20 February: hostile crowd; eggs, tomatoes, stones; children and students went in; they left that night in single file. Do not teach the ban as settled on 16 February. Curthoys later recalled that it took about six months before the council formally rescinded the rule (ABC News, 27 September 2019).

Route and Lismore. AIATSIS itinerary: 12 Sydney; 13 Wellington; 14 Gulargambone; 15 Walgett; 16 Moree (first); 17 Boggabilla; 18 Tenterfield, then back; 19–20 Moree (return); 21 Grafton; 22 Lismore and Cabbage Tree Island; 23 Bowraville; 24 Kempsey; 25 Taree; 26 Sydney. They did not leave for Lismore after the first Moree meeting. Some other lists add towns such as Warwick, Glen Innes, Inverell or Coffs Harbour; treat those as a minor conflict in the route lists.

Numbers. Twenty-nine left Sydney on 12 February (University of Sydney; Sydney Barani; AIATSIS). About 35 took part in all (AIATSIS).

By-law wording. The source draft shortened the rule. ABC News (22 February 2025) quotes a fuller wording, used in Super Sleuths with a content note. This file has not checked the 1955 council instrument itself. The statute is often dated 6 June 1955 (Curthoys).

Gary Williams. A Gumbaynggirr-Bundjalung student and SAFA member, not on the bus from Sydney and not at either Moree visit. Called home for a family emergency; joined at Bowraville on 23 February (University of Sydney, 2024; NSWALC; Sydney Barani; ABC News, 2025). Living: no dates on the student pages.

Perkins’s words and graduation. “The problem is out in the open now” is cited from AIATSIS as said at the 1965 FCAATSI conference. This file has not independently checked a 1965 transcript. “Second class is not good enough” has not been pinned to a 1965 primary source and is not used on the student pages. Do not call Perkins “the first Aboriginal person to graduate.” He graduated from the University of Sydney in 1966; Margaret Valadian graduated from the University of Queensland in the same year, the first acknowledged Aboriginal woman to complete an Australian university degree. Valadian is living: no dates on the student pages.

A misattributed quotation. The source draft gave Ann Curthoys the line that the ride “convinced a very wide section of the public that things were very, very bad.” That wording is Jim Spigelman’s, in a BBC interview published in 2015. It has been removed from her card.

The third witness. This file names Harry Hall, president of the Walgett Aborigines’ Progressive Association in 1965, rather than a group card. Do not conflate him with his son, Harry Phillip Hall, who is living, who also assisted in 1965, and who must not be given dates on a student page. Later Moree and Bowraville oral histories are options for a future revision after consultation, not extra cards in this draft.
